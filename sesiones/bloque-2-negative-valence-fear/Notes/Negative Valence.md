## Executive summary

Fear and threat can be understood through two complementary dimensional frameworks: RDoC and HiTOP. RDoC defines Negative Valence Systems as mechanisms responsible for responses to aversive situations or contexts, including fear, anxiety, and loss, and it separates threat responding into acute threat, potential threat, and sustained threat ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). HiTOP, in contrast, organizes psychopathology by empirical patterns of symptom covariation, placing fear-related syndromes within the broader internalizing spectrum ([HiTOP primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9122089/)). The key distinction is that RDoC starts from mechanisms and units of analysis, whereas HiTOP starts from clinical symptom dimensions and their hierarchical organization ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

The most useful class-level framing is to treat RDoC and HiTOP as different levels of explanation rather than competing systems. RDoC helps explain how organisms detect and respond to danger through constructs such as acute threat, potential threat, and sustained threat, while HiTOP helps explain how fear-related psychopathology clusters across disorders such as panic, phobias, social anxiety, agoraphobia, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). This interface matters because traditional DSM-style categories often obscure heterogeneity and comorbidity, whereas dimensional systems can model shared and distinct features across anxiety-related conditions ([HiTOP paradigm shift](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775140/)).

## Background: why dimensional models are needed

Traditional psychiatric diagnosis often treats disorders as categories, but fear, anxiety, and threat responses vary continuously across individuals and across clinical syndromes. HiTOP was developed as a dimensional classification intended to address arbitrary disorder boundaries, diagnostic unreliability, within-disorder heterogeneity, and comorbidity by organizing psychopathology around empirically derived dimensions ([HiTOP paradigm shift](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775140/)). RDoC was developed as a research framework that organizes mental functions into domains, constructs, and units of analysis, aiming to connect behavior, neurobiology, physiology, genetics, and self-report across normal-to-abnormal continua ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)).

The two frameworks therefore solve different problems. HiTOP gives clinical researchers a better map of symptom structure, whereas RDoC gives mechanistic researchers a way to study processes that cut across diagnostic categories ([HiTOP paradigm shift](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775140/)). Their integration is especially relevant for fear and threat because threat-related processes are both biologically tractable and clinically central to internalizing psychopathology ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

## RDoC Negative Valence Systems

RDoC defines Negative Valence Systems as systems primarily responsible for responses to aversive situations or contexts, such as fear, anxiety, and loss ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). Within this domain, RDoC separates negative valence into five constructs: Acute Threat, Potential Threat, Sustained Threat, Loss, and Frustrative Nonreward ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). This separation is important because “fear” is not treated as a single global emotion; it is decomposed by threat imminence, uncertainty, duration, and behavioral function ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)).

## Acute threat or fear

Acute Threat is the RDoC construct most directly aligned with fear. It refers to activation of the brain’s defensive motivational system to protect the organism from perceived danger, including responses to conditioned or unconditioned threat stimuli ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). In clinical research, markers of Acute Threat show robust associations with HiTOP Fear, especially panic disorder, agoraphobia, social phobia, specific phobias, and obsessive-compulsive disorder ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

This construct is valuable because it allows fear to be studied across multiple units of analysis rather than only through diagnosis. The RDoC-HiTOP interface review reports that physiological markers such as increased skin conductance and heart rate during defensive reactivity have been related to panic attacks and agoraphobia, while neural evidence links fear disorders to circuits involving the amygdala, insula, anterior cingulate, and prefrontal cortex ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). Behaviorally, avoidance, flight, and freezing are common markers of fear-related disorders, and paradigms such as CO2 challenge can capture individual differences in panic symptoms ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

## Potential threat or anxiety

Potential Threat differs from Acute Threat because the danger is uncertain, ambiguous, distant, or low in probability. RDoC describes this construct as involving enhanced risk assessment or vigilance in situations where harm may occur but is not immediately present ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). This distinction is clinically useful because fear often involves high-imminence defensive responding, whereas anxiety often involves sustained monitoring of uncertain danger ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)).

The empirical mapping is more complex than for Acute Threat. Enhanced startle potentiation to uncertain threat has been associated with fear disorders such as social anxiety disorder and specific phobia, and some neuroimaging evidence suggests that unpredictable threat engages anterior insula and brainstem-related responses across internalizing conditions ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). However, self-report markers such as anxiety sensitivity and intolerance of uncertainty may relate to both HiTOP Fear and HiTOP Distress, which means Potential Threat should not be reduced to a single clinical subfactor ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

## Sustained threat

Sustained Threat refers to an aversive emotional state caused by prolonged exposure to internal or external conditions that are actual or anticipated and adaptive to escape or avoid ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). RDoC emphasizes that sustained threat produces affective, cognitive, physiological, and behavioral changes that persist even in the absence of the immediate threat ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). This construct therefore moves from phasic fear toward chronic stress-related dysregulation.

In the RDoC-HiTOP interface, Sustained Threat is more strongly linked to HiTOP Distress than to HiTOP Fear. Individuals with high sustained threat show higher levels of distress symptoms, especially depression and posttraumatic stress, and distress psychopathology has been associated with markers such as punishment sensitivity, helpless behavior, anxious arousal, attentional bias, and childhood trauma exposure ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

## HiTOP: the Fear factor inside internalizing psychopathology

HiTOP conceptualizes psychopathology as a hierarchy of continuous dimensions rather than as discrete categories. At broad levels, HiTOP includes spectra such as internalizing, antagonistic externalizing, disinhibited externalizing, detachment, thought disorder, and somatoform, while narrower levels include subfactors, syndromes, symptom components, and maladaptive traits ([HiTOP primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9122089/)). The internalizing spectrum can be subdivided into more specific subfactors, including Fear and Distress ([HiTOP primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9122089/)).

The HiTOP Fear subfactor captures the shared variance among fear-related syndromes. In the HiTOP primer’s example, the fear dimension accounts for correlations among social phobia, panic, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms, while the distress dimension accounts for the association between depression and generalized anxiety ([HiTOP primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9122089/)). This hierarchy allows the same clinical problem to be represented at different levels: a narrow symptom such as situational avoidance, a syndrome such as social anxiety, a broader Fear subfactor, the Internalizing spectrum, or even the general factor of psychopathology ([HiTOP primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9122089/)).

For class discussion, the most important point is that HiTOP Fear is not identical to everyday fear. It is a clinical-statistical dimension that groups syndromes by covariation, whereas RDoC Acute Threat is a mechanistic construct intended to capture defensive responding to imminent danger across units of analysis ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). The overlap is strong but incomplete because clinical fear syndromes include learning, avoidance, context, cognition, arousal, impairment, and comorbidity beyond acute threat alone ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

## How RDoC and HiTOP connect

The strongest conceptual bridge is that RDoC Negative Valence Systems provide mechanistic constructs that can be studied in relation to HiTOP clinical dimensions. The RDoC-HiTOP interface review concludes that Negative Valence Systems are robustly and positively linked to the HiTOP Internalizing spectrum, consistent with the broader role of negative affectivity in internalizing psychopathology ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). Within that broad link, Acute Threat and Potential Threat tend to map more closely onto HiTOP Fear, whereas Sustained Threat and Loss tend to map more closely onto HiTOP Distress ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

This integration prevents a common mistake: assuming that one diagnosis maps neatly onto one mechanism. A person with panic disorder may show strong acute-threat physiology, but another person with the same diagnosis may be shaped more by distress, chronic stress, avoidance learning, or comorbidity ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). Dimensional frameworks are useful because they allow researchers and clinicians to ask whether the most relevant target is immediate defensive reactivity, uncertain-threat vigilance, sustained stress exposure, or a broader internalizing liability ([HiTOP paradigm shift](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775140/)).

## Clinical and research implications

For clinical research, the RDoC-HiTOP integration suggests that fear-related psychopathology should be assessed using both symptom dimensions and mechanism-linked measures. HiTOP can identify whether a patient or sample is primarily characterized by Fear, Distress, broader Internalizing, or more general psychopathology, while RDoC can guide selection of tasks, physiological markers, neural circuits, and self-report measures relevant to acute, potential, or sustained threat ([HiTOP primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9122089/); [NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). This approach may improve research precision because it avoids treating broad DSM categories as if they were mechanistically homogeneous ([HiTOP paradigm shift](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775140/)).

For treatment conceptualization, the distinction between Fear and Distress can shape targets. Fear-related presentations may involve situational triggers, avoidance, panic-like arousal, phobic cues, and defensive reactivity, whereas distress-related presentations may involve sustained negative affect, rumination, helplessness, trauma-related dysregulation, depression, and generalized anxiety ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). A transdiagnostic formulation can therefore ask whether the primary maintaining process is acute threat responding, uncertain-threat anticipation, chronic sustained threat, loss, or broader internalizing vulnerability ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs); [Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

## Limitations and cautions

The mapping between RDoC and HiTOP should not be treated as one-to-one. The RDoC-HiTOP interface review notes that fear conditioning evidence is less consistently aligned with HiTOP Fear than some other markers of Acute Threat, partly because fear conditioning may measure acquisition and learning rather than immediate threat response itself ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). Potential Threat is also complicated because physiological and neural markers often align with HiTOP Fear, whereas self-report markers such as anxiety sensitivity and intolerance of uncertainty may also align with HiTOP Distress ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)).

Another limitation is that HiTOP is primarily phenotypic, while RDoC is primarily mechanistic. HiTOP organizes symptoms and traits by empirical covariation, but it does not directly encode etiology, whereas RDoC emphasizes constructs linked to mechanisms and units of analysis but is less directly designed as a clinical diagnostic system ([HiTOP paradigm shift](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775140/)). The best use of both systems is therefore integrative: HiTOP identifies clinically meaningful dimensions, and RDoC tests the mechanisms that may explain those dimensions ([HiTOP paradigm shift](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775140/)).

## Conclusion

Fear and threat are best understood as transdiagnostic, dimensional phenomena. In RDoC, fear belongs mainly to the Negative Valence Systems domain and is separated into threat constructs based on immediacy, uncertainty, and duration, especially Acute Threat, Potential Threat, and Sustained Threat ([NIMH RDoC definitions](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/research/research-funded-by-nimh/rdoc/definitions-of-the-rdoc-domains-and-constructs)). In HiTOP, fear is a subfactor within the internalizing spectrum that captures the shared structure of clinical syndromes such as panic, phobias, social anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive symptoms ([HiTOP primer](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9122089/)).

The strongest synthesis is that RDoC explains the mechanisms of threat response, while HiTOP organizes the clinical expression of fear-related psychopathology. Acute and potential threat constructs generally converge with HiTOP Fear, whereas sustained threat and loss converge more strongly with HiTOP Distress ([Linking RDoC and HiTOP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8165014/)). For a class presentation or report, the key takeaway is that fear is not merely a symptom or a diagnosis; it is a multi-level construct that can be studied biologically, behaviorally, phenomenologically, and clinically across traditional disorder boundaries.
